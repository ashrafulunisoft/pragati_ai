Abstract Modern web-based enterprise applications
are increasingly vulnerable to brute-force, credential
stuffing, and automated bot attacks, yet traditional IPbased rate limiting and CAPTCHA mechanisms often
fail to adapt to evolving threats while frequently misidentifying legitimate users. This research proposes a novel
three-tier hybrid authentication architecture that integrates Redis-based real-time counters, a Model Context
Protocol (MCP) for MiniMax Large Language Model
(LLM)-driven contextual threat analysis, and an interpretable rule-based risk scoring algorithm that validates
AI decisions to reduce false positives. A threshold-based
invocation mechanism triggers LLM analysis selectively
40 of 186 events(≈ 21.5%) in the evalutaed configuration,
minimizing computational overhead and latency.If the
Minimax API goes down, the system does not just let
all traffic through. Instead, it uses a three-tier backup
plan based on risk levels; it blocks high-risk traffic (score
≥ 80), throttles medium-risk traffic using an N-strike
counter (score 50-79), and simply monitors low-risk traffic. Implemented within Laravel and validated through
106/106 predefined unit tests, the system achieves 83.9%
recall when the LLM augments the rule-based floor(up
from 21.2% with rules alone) at zero false positives over
49 legitimate negatives, demonstrating potential for deployment over conventional throttling approaches by
substantially lowering false positive rates. Furthermore,
the architecture is highly memory-efficient, using only
about 1.1 MB of Redis memory to track 10,000 active
entities at once. This low footprint suggests it will be
highly cost-effective for enterprise scale, though we still
need to run formal scalability benchmarks in the future.

1 Introduction
Laravel is one of the most popular PHP web frameworks, powering over 1.5 million websites worldwide [1]
and holding approximately a 64% market share among
PHP developers, according to the JetBrains State of
PHP 2025 survey [2]. It is widely used in multiple sectors including e-commerce, fintech, healthcare, CRMs,
and government applications, due to its robust security
features, scalability, and rapid development capabilities [3][4]. The widespread adoption of Laravel in sensitive and high-stakes domains makes its authentication
security a critical research concern for both industry
practitioners and the academic security community.
In the current digital era, web-based enterprise applications face increasingly sophisticated cyber threats,
including credential stuffing, brute-force attacks, and
bot-powered automated attacks. Traditional authentication security measures—such as fixed-window rate
limiting, IP-based blocking, and CAPTCHA—are fundamentally limited in their ability to provide contextaware decision-making. These conventional approaches
suffer from two critical weaknesses: first, they fail to
distinguish between legitimate users and attackers when
traffic patterns deviate from expected norms; second,
they lack the intelligence to adapt to evolving attack
strategies. Consequently, rightful users are frequently
and mistakenly blocked, while sophisticated attackers
can easily bypass these static defenses. This creates an
urgent need for authentication systems that are not only

2
rapid and scalable but also contextually intelligent and
explainable.
Recent research has demonstrated a rapid increase
in the adoption of AI-driven security systems. In particular, agentic AI systems and generative model-based
frameworks are being deployed in complex decisionmaking environments [5]. Additionally, several studies
have proposed the use of behavioral fingerprinting and
multi-layer security approaches in authentication systems [6,7]. However, these methods are generally designed for high-complexity environments with substantial computational resources and are not directly suitable
for lightweight, production-grade web-based authentication systems. The computational overhead and latency
introduced by these approaches make them impractical for real-time authentication scenarios. On the other
hand, in-memory data stores such as Redis have proven
highly effective for real-time security and fraud detection,
especially in high-throughput environments [8]. Redisbased systems provide exceptional performance with
microsecond-level response times, making them ideal for
traffic control and anomaly detection. However, these
systems are primarily limited to threshold-based monitoring and lack the capability to perform sophisticated
attack classification or provide intelligent, context-aware
decision-making support. They operate on predefined
rules that cannot adapt to novel attack patterns or
nuanced user behaviors. Recently, the Model Context
Protocol (MCP) has emerged as an important framework enabling context-aware integration between Large
Language Models (LLMs) and external systems. The potential and risks of MCP have been discussed in various
studies [9]. Security frameworks for MCP in multi-agent
AI systems have been proposed [10], along with research
on formal threat modeling and defense mechanisms [11].
Despite these advancements, the application of MCP in
authentication-level security remains limited, and this
area has not been sufficiently explored. To the best of our
knowledge, and based on the literature search described
in Section 2, no prior study we located experimentally
evaluates an MCP-enabled LLM combined with realtime threshold monitoring for authentication security in
production web environments. A significant research gap
therefore exists in developing a lightweight, explainable,
and context-aware authentication security system suitable for production web environments. Current solutions
either prioritize speed at the expense of intelligence or
provide sophisticated analysis at the cost of impractical
computational overhead.The integration of MCP with
authentication systems has received limited experimental evaluation, motivating our study of a lightweight,
explainable, and context-aware authentication security
system.

To address this gap, this research aims to answer
three key research questions. First, how can an effective
hybrid authentication security framework be designed
by integrating Redis-based threshold monitoring with
MCP-enabled LLM decision support in Laravel-based
web applications? Second, in what ways can thresholdbased counters and rule-based risk scoring be combined
with selectively invoked LLM analysis to enhance the
reliability, trustworthiness, and explainability of authentication decisions? Third, what is the effectiveness of the
proposed system in terms of detection accuracy, false positive rate, response time, and scalability when deployed
under simulated brute-force and credential-stuffing attack scenarios in real-world web environments?
We present a hybrid AI-driven secure authentication framework that integrates Redis-based threshold
monitoring with MCP-integrated LLM decision support. Our proposed system bridges the gap between
high-speed anomaly detection and intelligent threat reasoning. It goes beyond traditional anomaly detection by
enabling context-aware decision-making and explainable
risk scoring, making it highly suitable for productionlevel deployment. The key contributions of this work
are as follows: an explainable rule-based algorithm that
enables transparent and auditable security through predefined risk threshold enforcement, the rule score acts
as a floor that the AI decision can raise but never lower,
with bounded AI leniency, so security posture stays
consistent; a novel hybrid authentication architecture integrating Laravel/PHP, Redis, MCP, and MiniMax LLM
(with extensible support for models such as GLM 5.1 and
Kimi 2.6) for intelligent login security in production web
environments; introduction of adaptive IP- and emailbased rate limiting mechanisms for early detection and
mitigation of brute-force and malicious login attacks; development of MCP-LLM-driven contextual threat analysis that ranks risk and distinguishes bot-generated
requests from legitimate human users; implementation
of an intelligent response and alerting framework that
supports risk-based decisions and automatically notifies
administrators about suspicious authentication events;
enhanced authentication security through real-time risk
scoring and automated cyber threat reasoning, improving both detection capability and system robustness;
and design of a risk-adaptive three-tier degraded policy
where Redis counters are the always-on first line, the
MCP/LLM is invoked only above thresholds, and if the
MiniMax API fails the system fails closed at high risk
(score ≥ 80), applies an N-strike throttle at medium risk
(50–79), and monitors below, protecting legitimate users
while closing the fail-open hole.

3 Methodology
3.1 System Architecture
The overall architecture is a containerized Laravel-based
web application with dedicated modules for user interaction and secure authentication. The authentication
subsystem employs a layered defense: Redis-backed rate
limiting serves as the first line, followed by conditional
invocation of an AI analyzer via the MCP when predefined thresholds are exceeded. Figure 1 illustrates the
system architecture.

3.2 Model Context Protocol (MCP)
Our MCP layer implements the Model Context Protocol
specification 2025-06-18 over Streamable HTTP transport with JSON-RPC 2.0. MCP functions as a service
layer that:

The implementation stack is summarized as follows:
Laravel 12 (PHP 8.4) serves as the backend framework
and handles application routing and core business logic;
MySQL 8.0 provides persistent storage for user credentials and logs; Redis 7.x supplies high-speed rate

limiting and temporary IP blocking; and MiniMax-M2.1
performs behavioral analysis and threat classification.
This stack provides the necessary throughput for
real-time authentication while supporting the computational overhead of LLM integration. Since the attackerinfluenced email address and IP are read before controller validation, the outbound prompt is defended
with four layers. L1 sanitises inputs (control characters
stripped, length-bounded, format-validated; malformed
identifiers are dropped). L2 skips AI analysis entirely
for malformed identifiers, deferring to controller validation (HTTP 422). L3 wraps all dynamic context
between an uncloseable <context>/</context> fence
with explicit ‘UNTRUSTED DATA, never instructions’
rules; angle brackets inside identifiers are neutralised
so the fence can never be closed early, and counters
are rendered only as integers so no string can be smuggled into the prompt. L4 is a deterministic rule-floor
guardrail: the F1–F5 fusion (Algorithm 1) ensures even
a fully-compromised model returning SAFE at risk 0
and confidence 1.0 cannot flip a ≥80 verdict and cannot
obtain a full allow inside the 50–79 band (best case:

a SUSPICIOUS step-up). Output parsing is hardened
likewise — JSON is extracted from prose, decisions validated against {SAFE, SUSPICIOUS, MALICIOUS},
and non-numeric risk/confidence are treated as absent.
Worst-case injection is measured in the adversarial mode
of Section 5.4, where recall holds at 79.6% at FPR 0.
"Outbound context is pseudonymized so that raw
PII never reaches the third-party LLM: the email is
replaced by a stable, non-reversible HMAC-SHA256
identifier (email_id:...) and the IP by its coarse network prefix plus an HMAC (/24 for IPv4, /64 for IPv6 —
ip_net:...), keyed with a server-side salt that is never
disclosed. Pseudonyms are stable across attempts (repeat offenders remain recognisable) and non-reversible
without the salt; only coarse locality and integer attempt counts cross the outbound boundary, on both the
direct API call and the MCP tools/call arguments.
Detection semantics are unchanged because decisions
are driven by the counters, not the identifiers. Raw
identifiers persist only in local audit logs and Redis, by
design."
4 MCP-Based Secure Login System
4.1 Redis Behavioral Tracking
To mitigate brute-force attacks, the system employs
a high-performance tracking mechanism based on Redis in-memory data structures. This approach utilizes
fixed-window counters (a single 15-minute window) to
monitor login attempt frequencies for both IP addresses
and email identifiers. The operational workflow of the
proposed MCP-based malicious login detection mechanism comprises the following sequential phases:
Login Request Initiation: The process begins
when a user submits authentication credentials via the
POST /login endpoint. The request is intercepted by
the McpMaliciousLoginMiddleware prior to reaching
the core authentication module, acting as a gatekeeper.
Feature Extraction: Upon interception, the middleware extracts critical request attributes, specifically
the client IP address and the submitted email identifier.
These attributes serve as primary telemetry features for
subsequent risk evaluation.
Rate Limit Check: The system queries Redisbased telemetry counters—specifically attack:login:
ip:{ip} and attack:login:email:{email}—to
retrieve failed login attempt frequencies within a fixed
15-minute window.
Threshold Evaluation: The system evaluates the
retrieved counts against predefined security thresholds.
If the IP-based attempt count exceeds θip = 5 or the
email-based attempt count exceeds θemail = 3, the login

MCP Invocation: For escalated requests, the McpSecurityService constructs a structured context comprising telemetry statistics and temporal behavior patterns.

AI-Based Risk Analysis: The language model
evaluates the provided contextual frameworks and classifies the login attempt as MALICIOUS or SAFE
based on inferred behavioral risk.
Decision Enforcement: If the classification result
is MALICIOUS, the originating IP address is temporarily blocked by setting the Redis key blocked:ip:{ip}
with a time-to-live (TTL) of 3600 seconds, and an HTTP
403 (Forbidden) response is returned. If classified as
SAFE, the request is forwarded to Laravel’s native authentication pipeline for standard credential verification.

4.2 MCP AI Risk Analysis
When predefined thresholds are exceeded, the login behavior is analyzed using MiniMax AI connected to MCP.
When threshold conditions are met, the system invokes
the MCP service for contextual analysis. The algorithm
for this process is formalized in Algorithm 2. The system
computes a quantitative risk score between 0 and 100
based on behavioral patterns.

labelled F1–F5 in the audit log and throughout this paper (Table 2). The effective score is S = max(Srule , SAI ),
so the AI can raise the score but never lower it, and
every rule that permits a request is evaluated only after
the deterministic guardrail F1 has been checked.

read from configuration rather than hard-coded. In the

4.4 Enforcement Mechanisms

Atomic Redis

Atomic fixed-window counters. Every counter is
updated by the Lua script in Listing 1, so INCR and
EXPIRE execute as a single atomic operation. No crash
can leave a key without a TTL, and EXPIRE is issued
only when the window opens, which gives a true fixed
15-minute window instead of a rolling accumulator that
never decays under sustained probing. The cache fallback
uses the same fixed-window semantics.
Shared-NAT-aware blocking. Each successful
login records the account in a distinct set keyed by
IP. When at least θNAT = 3 distinct accounts have
logged in successfully from an address, it is treated as
shared (office, campus or mobile network) and a block
is scoped to the attacked e-mail instead of the whole
IP (Algorithm 1, lines 40–44). Both block scopes are
checked before any AI call (line 1), so already-blocked
identifiers cost no billable LLM invocations. The residual
trade-off is discussed in Section 6.
Enforced step-up challenges. Recommended
CAPTCHA/OTP actions are enforced rather than advisory. The middleware answers with HTTP 428 and
a random 128-bit single-use token; the CAPTCHA is
a server-generated arithmetic question and the OTP
is a six-digit code delivered out of band (by e-mail, or
logged in the test configuration) and never returned in
the response. Answers are stored only as salted SHA-256
hashes and compared in constant time. A token expires
after 300 s, allows at most three answer attempts and is
deleted on first success, and challenge issuance is limited
to 10 per IP per 15 minutes to prevent challenge-based
denial of service. Scripted traffic that cannot solve a
challenge stops at HTTP 428, which is what bounds the
damage under a compromised AI (Section 5.6).

6 Threat Model
The system is analysed against the following threat model.
Assets. Compromise of the six assets below carries the following impact. User accounts (credentials): account takeover,
exposure of PII, claims or orders. Account availability: legitimate users locked out (denial of service). Redis security state:
bypass or weaponisation of blocks. LLM API key/quota: cost
exhaustion or AI-layer outage. Login behaviour context (IP,
email): privacy risk from PII leakage to a third party. MCP
endpoint /api/mcp: an unauthorised security-analysis oracle.
Attacker capabilities (C1–C11). The attacker is assumed capable of: unlimited online credential guessing from
their own machines (C1); rotating source IPs via proxy, VPN,
or botnet (C2); rotating target accounts using known or leaked
email lists (C3); knowing every threshold, weight, and policy,
since the code and this paper are assumed public (C4); crafting
arbitrary email payloads for prompt injection (C5); keeping
their rate below the invocation thresholds, i.e. low-and-slow
(C8); and sharing an IP with legitimate victims via NAT (C9).
Two capabilities are conditional: spoofing X-Forwarded-For succeeds only if upstream proxies are trusted, and is untrusted
on public-facing interfaces (C6); and the attacker can only
partially withhold or induce AI-layer failures, by saturating or
exploiting third-party outages (C7). Two capabilities are explicitly excluded: intercepting client-to-server traffic, since TLS
is assumed (C10); and compromising the server, Redis, or the
MiniMax account, which sits outside the infrastructure trust
boundary (C11).

5.5 Redis Memory Optimization

6.1 Trusted components.

Memory efficiency is critical for production deployment. By using
compact key naming and appropriate TTL values, the system
achieves a minimal memory footprint: the attack:login:ip:*
and attack:login:email:* counters use a 900 s TTL (72 B and
88 B per key respectively), and blocked:ip:* uses a 3600 s TTL
(64 B per key).
For a realistic production scenario with 10,000 concurrent
IP addresses and 5,000 email addresses, the estimated total
Redis memory consumption is approximately 1.1 MB, computed from per-key sizes (72 B for attack:login:ip:*, 88 B
for attack:login:email:*, and 64 B for blocked:ip:*). This
figure is an arithmetic estimate rather than an active empirical
measurement; a comprehensive scalability experiment tracking
from 1K to 10M entities to systematically evaluate memory
footprint, fragmentation ratios, and throughput performance
(ops/sec) is planned as future work.

The Laravel application server and Redis are trusted (they
run the policy; a Redis compromise means full security-state
loss); the cache fallback is trusted but with weaker per-node
consistency; /api/mcp is trusted only with a valid Bearer token;
the MiniMax LLM is semi-trusted — it sees pseudonymised
context only and its verdict is advisory, because deterministic
rules bound every decision; the email channel is trusted for admin
alerts; the client and network are untrusted (TLS terminates at
the server).

8 Conclusion

9.4 Ethics Statement

This research presents a risk-adaptive hybrid authentication
architecture that uses deterministic security controls (threshold
counters + rule-based scoring) for fast-path enforcement and
selectively invokes an LLM through MCP for contextual analysis,
with independently evaluated fallback and adversarial-resilience
mechanisms. Evaluation shows the LLM measurably raises recall
from 21.2% (rules only) to 83.9% at zero false positives over 49
legitimate negatives, with a tiered degradation — not blanket
fail-open — policy during AI or Redis failures, while a threat
model formalises the associated guarantees and residual risks.
What remains before production deployment is the empirical
validation discussed below.
We acknowledge the following limitations and open items.
Detection was evaluated on a reproducible 186-event simulation;
validation against 10k+ real or trace-based events remains open.
A load/stress benchmark (e.g., k6 or wrk) against a deployed instance — measuring throughput and latency under concurrency
— has not yet been run; accordingly, we report Redis-command
latency separately from full-request latency and plan independent p50 /p95 /p99 across ≥ 100 live calls. The feature set remains
narrow (IP network, pseudonymized email, attempt counts, time
window); a genuine multi-feature behavioral model (user-agent,
fingerprint, ASN, timing statistics) and a multi-class attack-type
classifier are future work. Low-and-slow attacks that stay below
the invocation thresholds are inherently undetected (measured
recall 0 in all modes) and would require additional features or
a honeypot/UEBA layer. Threshold selection via ROC/PR on
held-out splits is open. MCP transport-level client spoofing and
request replay (beyond the replay-protected challenge tokens),
as well as graded AI-latency degradation between 100 ms and
3 s, have not yet been evaluated. Finally, while an early blockexit avoids re-analysing already-blocked identifiers, first-contact
challenged traffic still consumes LLM calls; verdict caching is
the planned mitigation.

This study involved no human participants, human data or
animals. All login events used for evaluation were generated by
a seeded simulator.
